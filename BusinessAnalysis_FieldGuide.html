The Business Analyst Field Guide

You are not training to become a business analyst. You are training to direct, evaluate and approve the requirements an AI drafts for you — and to know, in the thirty seconds before you sign, whether what you are reading will hold up in build, in test, and in front of a CFO. This guide carries the working twenty percent, then shows you nine complete deliverables for one invented but believable initiative.

A reference you keep open in a second tab while an AI drafts requirements for you. Every concept is followed immediately by the artifact it produces, filled in for a real-shaped initiative.

What it is not

Not IIBA certification prep, and not a template pack. Templates without a worked example teach you the shape of a document but not the judgement to accept or reject its contents.

The premise of this week

An AI can produce a plausible Business Requirements Document in ninety seconds. Plausible is the problem. It will invent a stakeholder who does not exist, mark eleven of thirteen requirements as Must, write acceptance criteria that no test can fail, and quietly leave a requirement with no owner, no measure and no test. Your job as the architect is to catch all four of those in one pass. That skill is what the rest of this page builds.

Orientation · 02

Why business analysis exists

Business analysis exists because the cost of being wrong is not distributed evenly across a project. It is almost free at the start and ruinous at the end.

Every system is built twice: once in language, and once in code. Business analysis is the discipline of building it correctly the first time, in language, while changing your mind is still cheap. A misunderstanding that costs one hour to correct in a requirements conversation costs a hundred hours to correct after it has been designed around, coded against, tested, documented, trained on, and shipped to forty-two restaurants.

The four failures a BA is paid to prevent

1. Building the wrong thing correctly

The system passes every test and nobody uses it. This is the most expensive failure mode in enterprise AI, because the model works, the pipeline works, and the failure is invisible until adoption data arrives six months later.

2. Unstated assumptions

Everyone in the room agrees on “accurate forecast” and nobody agrees on what accurate means, at what grain, measured how, against what baseline. The disagreement surfaces at user acceptance testing.

3. Scope that grows silently

No single change is unreasonable. The forty-first is fatal. Without a prioritized, traceable baseline, there is no defensible way to say “that is real, and it is not in this release.”

4. Unverifiable success

The project ends and nobody can say whether it worked. If the baseline was never measured before go-live, the benefit can never be proved afterwards, and the next initiative will be funded on faith.

Why this matters more with AI, not less

Traditional software fails loudly: it throws an exception. An AI system fails quietly and confidently — it returns a prep quantity of 40 pounds where the right answer was 12, in exactly the same format, with exactly the same tone. Because the failure has no signature, the requirement must carry the detection: an accuracy threshold, a confidence band, a fallback path, an override log. Requirements written for deterministic software are not sufficient for probabilistic software. This is the single most important adjustment you will make as an AI Solution Architect.

Orientation · 03

The twenty percent that matters

Seven concepts. Everything else in the discipline is elaboration on these, and you can direct a competent AI to produce every artifact in this guide once you hold them.

1. Functional vs. non-functional requirements

A functional requirement says what the system does. A non-functional requirement (NFR) says how well it must do it, under what constraints. Functional requirements get written by default; non-functional requirements get forgotten, and then get discovered in production at three in the morning.

The AI-specific NFRs you must always demand

Generic NFR checklists predate machine learning. For any AI-bearing system, four more are mandatory, and an AI drafting your requirements will rarely volunteer them:

  • Accuracy floor — a named metric, a grain, a threshold, and a measurement window. “Item-day WAPE at or below 12.0%, measured on a rolling 28-day window, per location.”
  • Fallback behaviour — what the system does when the model, the feature store, or an upstream feed is unavailable. Silence is not an acceptable answer.
  • Explainability — what the user is shown about why a number was produced, and at what level of detail.
  • Human override and its audit trail — who may overrule the model, how, whether a reason is captured, and how long the record is kept.

2. User stories

A user story is a placeholder for a conversation, written from the point of view of someone who benefits. The canonical form is deliberately restrictive, because the restriction is what forces clarity:

As a <specific role>, I want <a capability>, so that <a measurable outcome>.

The third clause is where the value hides, and it is the clause AI drafts most often degrade into tautology. “So that I can generate a prep plan” restates the capability. “So that I stop over-prepping proteins that get thrown away at close” names the outcome, and can be measured. When you review, read the third clause first: if it does not survive the question “so what?”, send the story back.

A well-formed story satisfies INVEST: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable. Of the six, the two that catch the most AI-generated defects are Small (a story that takes a whole release is an epic wearing a disguise) and Testable (if you cannot describe the test, the story is a wish).

3. Acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria are the conditions under which a story is agreed to be done. They convert intent into a pass/fail decision that a tester, a developer and a stakeholder will all read the same way. The Given/When/Then form is the industry standard because it separates the three things people conflate:

GIVEN  the state of the world before the behaviour — preconditions, data, permissions

WHEN   the single triggering action or event

THEN   the observable, verifiable result — including what is stored, shown, logged or sent

Rules that make criteria hold up in review: one behaviour per criterion; no conjunctions hiding a second requirement in the When; observable results only (“the user understands the recommendation” is not observable, “the top three drivers are displayed with their percentage contribution” is); and at least one criterion covering the unhappy path. A story with only happy-path criteria is half a story — and it is exactly what an AI will hand you unless you ask otherwise.

4. Personas

A persona is a compressed, evidence-based model of a user, written so a team can make decisions on someone’s behalf without calling a meeting. The useful content is goals, frustrations, context of use, and the decision they are trying to make. The useless content is demographics. Knowing that a kitchen manager is 34 with two children tells you nothing; knowing that she reviews tomorrow’s prep plan on a grease-splashed tablet in a 300-lux kitchen at 21:40 with two minutes to spare, and that she will silently abandon anything requiring more than three taps, changes the entire interface.

Personas earn their place when they end arguments. “Would Dana do this at 21:40?” is a faster and better question than “is this good UX?” See document D5 for four written to that standard.

5. Use cases

Where a user story is a sentence, a use case is a script. It names an actor, a goal, preconditions, a numbered main flow, and — the part that matters — the alternate and exception flows. Use cases are the right instrument whenever a workflow has branching, multiple actors, system-to-system steps, or failure modes that need explicit handling. That describes every AI integration.

The discipline in a use case is not the happy path; anyone can write the happy path. It is asking, at every numbered step, “what else could happen here?” and writing that down as 3a, 3b, 3c. When you review an AI-drafted use case, count the alternate flows. Fewer than one per two main-flow steps in an integration-heavy workflow means the failure analysis was not done.

6. Prioritization

Covered in full in section 05. The short version: a requirement set without priorities is not a requirement set, it is a wish list, and it transfers all scoping risk from the business onto the build team.

7. Traceability

Traceability is the property that every requirement can be followed forwards to the test that proves it and backwards to the objective that justifies it. It is maintained in a Requirements Traceability Matrix (D8) and it answers three questions nothing else can:

  • Coverage — is every objective actually being delivered by something?
  • Orphans — is anything being built that no objective asked for? Orphan requirements are how a six-month project becomes a nine-month project.
  • Impact — if this requirement changes, what design, code and tests move with it? Without the matrix, this question is answered by memory, and memory leaves the company.
Orientation · 04

Good vs. bad requirements

A bad requirement is rarely wrong. It is usually vague in a way that lets everyone agree in the room and disagree in production.

The seven tests

Run every requirement through these. A requirement that fails any one of them goes back, no matter how confidently it was written.

TestThe questionWhat failure sounds like
UnambiguousCan two competent people read this and build different things?“The system should handle high volume appropriately.”
TestableCan I write a test that fails if this is not met?“The interface must be intuitive and user-friendly.”
NecessaryWhich objective dies if we drop it?“The system shall support export to XML.” (Nobody asked. Nobody will use it.)
FeasibleCan it be built with what we have, in the time we have?“Forecast accuracy of 99% at item level for new menu items.”
AtomicIs there exactly one requirement here?“The system shall forecast demand and generate purchase orders and notify the district manager.”
PrioritizedWhat happens to the release if this slips?Every line marked MUST.
TraceableWhich objective is this serving, and which test proves it?A requirement with an empty Source column.

Rewrites, from the worked example

Weak
The system should produce accurate demand forecasts for each restaurant.

No metric, no grain, no threshold, no measurement window, no baseline. “Accurate” is unfalsifiable, so this requirement can never fail a test — which means it can never pass one either. It will be marked complete on the developer’s word.

Strong
FR-03 / NFR-A1. The forecasting service shall produce a next-day demand quantity for every active menu item at every location, achieving a weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE) at or below 12.0% at the item-day grain, measured on a rolling 28-day window per location, against a baseline of 22.4% established in the 90 days before go-live.

Named metric, explicit grain, numeric threshold, measurement window, stated baseline. A dashboard can turn red on this. A contract can be written against it.

Weak
Kitchen managers should be able to adjust the plan if they disagree with it.

“Should be able to” hides the whole design. Adjust by how much? With what authority? Does the system learn from it? Is the override recorded? Who sees it? This single sentence conceals four separate requirements and an entire governance decision.

Strong
FR-08. A user holding the Kitchen Manager role shall be able to override the recommended prep quantity for any item on their own location’s plan, up to ±100% of the recommendation. Overrides beyond ±100% require District Manager approval. Every override shall capture a reason code from a controlled list of eight values, and shall be written to the override audit log with user, timestamp, original value, new value and reason, retained for 24 months.

One behaviour, bounded authority, a named escalation, a controlled vocabulary, an audit record with a retention period. Testable line by line.

Weak
The system must be highly available and performant.

Two NFRs fused into a slogan. Neither is measurable. Nobody has said available when — a restaurant system that is down between 02:00 and 04:00 is fine; down at 16:00 on a Friday is a crisis.

Strong
NFR-P2. The prep-plan interface shall return a regenerated single-location plan within 4.0 seconds at P95 and 8.0 seconds at P99, measured at the edge, at a load of 42 concurrent locations.

NFR-R1. The service shall maintain 99.5% availability during the operating window 05:00–23:00 local time per location. Outside that window, the target is 95%. Planned maintenance is excluded and shall be scheduled between 02:00 and 04:00 local.

Split into two requirements, each with a number, a percentile, a measurement point, a load assumption and an operating window.

Words that should trigger a rejection

When these appear in a requirement, the requirement is not finished: appropriate, adequate, sufficient, robust, seamless, user-friendly, intuitive, flexible, scalable (without a number), fast, real-time (without a latency), as needed, where applicable, etc., and/or, minimize, maximize, optimize, improve, support, handle, manage. Each of these words moves a decision from the requirement into someone’s head during the build. Ask “compared to what, measured how?” and rewrite.

Orientation · 05

Prioritization with MoSCoW

MoSCoW works only if the definitions are enforced. Its entire value is the argument it forces before the build, rather than during it.

LevelDefinition — enforce this exactlyWorked example
MUST Without it, the release is not viable and should not ship. The correct test is: would we delay go-live rather than ship without this? If the honest answer is no, it is not a Must. Generate a next-day prep plan for every active item at every location. Without this there is no product.
SHOULD Important and painful to omit, but there is a workaround the business can live with for one release. Ships if the schedule holds. Auto-drafted purchase orders to the distributor. Painful to omit — managers keep ordering by hand for one more quarter — but the forecast still delivers most of its value.
COULD Desirable, low cost, genuinely first to be dropped when time is short. Nobody escalates if it disappears. A weather-driver overlay on the item detail panel. Nice, explanatory, entirely optional.
WON'T Explicitly out of scope for this release, recorded rather than deleted. This is the most valuable of the four, because it is the written record of a decision that will otherwise be re-litigated monthly. Automatic menu-price optimization. Real ambition, wrong release, and it drags in finance and legal review.

The distribution rule

A healthy MoSCoW split puts roughly a third of effort in Must, and no more than half. When more than 60% of requirements are Musts, prioritization has not happened — the list has simply been relabelled, and the team has lost its only lever for absorbing a schedule shock. This is the single most common defect in AI-generated requirement sets, because a language model has no cost model and no schedule to protect: everything it writes feels essential.

Two questions that break a fake Must

Ask the sponsor: “If this one item were not ready on go-live day, would you delay the whole launch for it?” and “What does the business do on day one if this is missing?” If there is an answer to the second that does not involve stopping work, the item is a Should. Run this on the top ten Musts in any AI-drafted set and expect three to move.

Orientation · 06

KPIs and measurement

A requirement without a measure is an opinion. A KPI without a baseline is a number nobody can argue with, and therefore a number nobody can learn from.

The five parts of a usable KPI

Every KPI in a requirements document must carry all five. Missing any one makes it undeliverable, and AI drafts routinely supply only the first two.

1. Name and definition

The exact formula, including numerator, denominator and exclusions. “Food waste percentage” is a name; “value of logged waste ÷ total food purchases, excluding transfers between locations” is a definition.

2. Baseline

The measured current value, with the period it was measured over. If it has not been measured before go-live, it cannot be claimed afterwards.

3. Target and date

The value that constitutes success, and when it must be true by. “Improve waste” is not a target; “≤4.5% by 31 March 2027” is.

4. Source and owner

The system of record the number comes from, and the named human accountable for the result. Ownership by a committee is ownership by nobody.

5. Frequency and grain

How often it is computed and at what level — per item-day, per location-week, per group-month. Grain disputes are the most common cause of two dashboards disagreeing.

And one guard rail

Every efficiency KPI needs a paired quality KPI. Cut waste without watching stockouts and you will hit the target by running out of food.

The four defects an AI draft will almost always contain

Confident invention

A stakeholder, a system name, an integration or a regulation that does not exist in your organisation, stated with the same authority as the true parts. Check every proper noun against reality before you read anything else.

Priority inflation

Nearly everything marked Must, because the model has no schedule to protect and no budget to spend. Re-derive priority yourself with the two questions in section 05.

Untestable padding

Fluent sentences with no falsifiable content — “the solution shall provide a seamless and intuitive experience”. Delete on sight; do not negotiate with it.

Missing failure analysis

The happy path is complete and elegant; the degraded path, the cold-start case, the bad-data case and the override case are absent. Ask for them explicitly, by name.

The thirty-second triage

If you only have half a minute before a meeting: count the Musts, search the document for a digit in every NFR, look for the word “fallback”, and check that the traceability matrix has no empty cells. Those four probes catch the majority of what is wrong with a typical AI-drafted package.

Orientation · 08

Directing an AI to draft requirements

The quality of an AI-drafted requirement set is set almost entirely by the constraints in the instruction. Ask for “a BRD” and you get a brochure. Ask with the constraints below and you get something reviewable.

The instruction pattern

Five elements, every time. Each one closes a specific failure mode.

ElementWhat you supplyFailure it prevents
GroundingThe real organisation, real systems by name, real volumes, and an explicit instruction: “do not invent systems, stakeholders or figures; mark anything you need to assume as ASSUMPTION.”Confident invention.
StructureThe exact sections and the exact identifier scheme (BR-nn, FR-nn, NFR-Xn, US-nn, AC-nn.n, TC-nn.n).Untraceable output that cannot be cross-referenced.
Quotas and constraints“No more than 40% Must. Every NFR must contain a number. Every story needs at least one negative acceptance criterion. Every use case needs at least two alternate flows.”Priority inflation, vague NFRs, happy-path-only thinking.
MeasurementThe baselines you actually have, and the instruction to leave a baseline blank rather than estimate it.Fabricated baselines — the most dangerous defect, because it is invisible and it invalidates the business case.
Self-check“End with a table listing every requirement that has no test, every objective with no requirement, and every assumption you made.”Orphans and silent assumptions.

A worked instruction

Draft a Business Requirements Document for Project Mise en Place at Harbor & Vine Hospitality Group: 42 casual-dining restaurants in Ohio, Michigan and Indiana; FY25 revenue $128.4M; food purchases $40.3M.

Systems that exist and may be referenced — and no others: Northstar POS, MenuCore recipe master, ShiftPilot labour scheduler, Ledgerline ERP, Great Lakes Provisions EDI ordering. Do not invent systems, stakeholders, regulations or figures. Where you need a fact I have not given you, write ASSUMPTION and state it in the assumptions register.

Structure: 1 Purpose, 2 Background and problem, 3 Objectives with KPIs, 4 Scope in and out, 5 Stakeholders, 6 Business requirements BR-nn, 7 Functional requirements FR-nn, 8 Non-functional requirements NFR-Xn, 9 Data requirements, 10 Assumptions, 11 Constraints, 12 Risks, 13 Dependencies, 14 Acceptance and sign-off.

Constraints: MoSCoW on every requirement, no more than 50% Must; every NFR contains a number, a percentile or a window; each requirement names its source stakeholder and the objective it serves; include degraded-mode, cold-start and override behaviour as explicit requirements; the baselines are WAPE 22.4%, waste 6.8% of purchases, 3.2 stockouts per location-week, 5.5 admin hours per location-week — do not invent others.

Finish with three tables: requirements with no linked objective; objectives with no linked requirement; assumptions made.

Then review, do not read

When the draft returns, do not read it front to back — that is how fluent prose defeats you. Work the checks in section 07 in order, out of document order: count Musts first, grep for digits in the NFR section, jump straight to the traceability tables at the end, verify every proper noun. Read the prose last, once the structure has already been proved sound.

The one thing you must never delegate

The baseline numbers, and the definition of success. An AI can draft every requirement, every use case and every test in this guide. It cannot tell you what the waste percentage really is today, and if you let it estimate one, every downstream financial figure in the business case becomes fiction — fiction that will be quoted back to you by a CFO nine months later.

Worked example · 09

Project Mise en Place

One invented but believable initiative, carried consistently through all nine documents. Every number below appears again, unchanged, in the business case, the requirements, the stories and the traceability matrix — which is itself the point of the exercise.

Fictional company, real shape

Harbor & Vine Hospitality Group does not exist. The operating figures, cost structure and system landscape are constructed to be representative of a mid-market multi-unit restaurant group, so that the documents behave like real deliverables. Use them as a model of form and rigour, not as industry benchmarks.

The organisation

Harbor & Vine Hospitality Group operates 42 casual-dining restaurants across Ohio, Michigan and Indiana under a single coastal-comfort concept. FY25 revenue was $128.4M; food purchases were $40.3M, or 31.4% of revenue. A typical location runs a 96-item menu built from roughly 340 ingredients, and employs one kitchen manager, two prep leads and eleven line staff.

Ordering and prep planning are done by hand. Each Sunday and Wednesday, kitchen managers estimate next-period demand from last week’s tickets, a paper prep sheet and personal judgement, then place orders with Great Lakes Provisions through the distributor’s web portal.

The problem, measured

  • 6.8% of food purchases are logged as waste — $2.74M a year.
  • 3.2 items per location per week are 86’d (run out) during service, costing an estimated $0.31M a year in lost contribution and measurable guest dissatisfaction.
  • Kitchen managers spend 5.5 hours a week on prep sheets and ordering — 12,012 hours a year across the group, at a loaded cost of $0.37M.
  • Manual next-day demand estimates carry a 22.4% item-day WAPE, measured over the 90 days to 30 June 2026.
  • Waste and stockouts move in opposite directions: managers who protect against 86’s over-prep, and managers who protect against waste run out.

The initiative

Project Mise en Place introduces a demand-forecasting and prep-planning service that produces, for every location every evening, a recommended prep quantity for each active menu item and a draft purchase order for the next delivery window. Kitchen managers review, adjust within bounded authority, and confirm. Actual usage and logged waste flow back nightly, and the model is retrained weekly. The manual prep sheet is decommissioned per location thirty days after that location reaches steady state.

1. Problem statement

Harbor & Vine spends $40.3M a year on food to produce $128.4M of revenue. Of that spend, $2.74M is thrown away and a further $0.31M of contribution is lost to items running out mid-service. Both figures are the product of the same root cause: forty-two individuals estimating tomorrow’s demand by hand, each with a different method, none of them measurable, and all of them working from a single week of history held largely in memory.

The group has already taken the cheap actions. Portion control was tightened in FY24. Prep sheets were standardised in FY25. Neither moved food cost, because neither addressed the estimate itself. Food cost has moved from 30.9% to 31.4% of revenue over two years while menu prices rose 6.1%, which means the underlying operational loss is growing, not shrinking.

2. Vision statement

For kitchen managers and district managers at Harbor & Vine, who must decide every day how much of each item to prepare and order, Project Mise en Place is a demand-forecasting and prep-planning service that produces a defensible, explainable recommended quantity for every item, every location, every day. Unlike the current manual prep sheet, which depends entirely on individual memory and is invisible to management, our solution is measurable, consistent across the estate, improves weekly from its own errors, and leaves the manager in charge of the final call.

The intent is explicitly advisory. The system is not being built to replace judgement; it is being built to give judgement a better starting point and to make the resulting decisions visible, comparable and improvable across forty-two kitchens.

8. Risks to the case

  • Benefit attribution. The concurrent Menu Engineering initiative also claims waste reduction. Without the attribution rule in DEP-05, both initiatives will report the same dollars. Finance owns the rule; it must be agreed before the first pilot location goes live, not after.
  • Adoption, not accuracy, is the binding constraint. A perfectly accurate forecast that managers override on 60% of lines delivers no benefit whatsoever. The override rate is therefore tracked weekly from day one of the pilot as the leading indicator for the entire case.
  • Baseline representativeness. The 90-day baseline (A-03) spans April to June. If seasonality makes that window unrepresentative, both the problem size and the benefit shift. A twelve-month retrospective rebaseline runs in parallel during build at no additional cost.
  • Waste posting discipline. Seven locations post waste weekly rather than daily (DR-03). Their benefit cannot be measured at the required grain until that practice changes, which is a change-management dependency rather than a technical one.

9. Recommendation

Approve Option 2 at $535K one-time and $200K per annum, with the phased deployment described in D7 and a formal stage gate at pilot exit. The gate criteria are: item-day WAPE at or below 15.0% across the pilot cohort over 28 consecutive days; plan acceptance at or above 60%; and no severity-1 defect open. Failure at the gate stops the rollout with $312K of the $535K committed, which is the deliberate purpose of taking the pilot first.

Goal. Produce a complete, explainable prep plan for every in-scope location for the next business date, before staff arrive.

  3. The service assembles the feature vector for each active item for the business date.

  6. The service applies par-level guard rails where configured, flagging any clamped line.
  7. The service explodes item demand to ingredient prep quantities using the bill of materials effective on the business date.
  8. The service writes the plan under the idempotency key (location_id, business_date, model_version).
  9. The service publishes the plan and notifies the kitchen manager by 05:00 local.
  10. The service records run duration, line count and feature snapshot reference.

Evidence base

These personas were built from 12 site visits across three districts and 19 interviews conducted between March and May 2026, plus 90 days of override-free baseline observation of how prep sheets are actually completed. Every frustration listed below was stated by at least three separate individuals. A persona assembled from imagination is a liability; it lends the authority of research to whatever the author already believed.

Dana Whitfield — Kitchen Manager  PRIMARY

Represents 42 people · uses the system twice daily · the single most important user of this system

Context of use. Reviews tomorrow’s plan at roughly 21:40, standing, on a wall-mounted tablet in a kitchen at about 300 lux, with wet or gloved hands, while the closing shift works around her. She has between ninety seconds and four minutes. She reviews again at 06:30 while receiving a delivery. She has never opened the desktop version of anything.

What she is actually deciding. Not “is this forecast correct” — she has no way to know that. She is deciding “is this number close enough that I do not need to think about this item tonight?” Every design decision in the review interface should serve that question.

Goals
  • Never run out of a signature item during service — this is the failure her district manager will hear about
  • Leave the building at a predictable hour
  • Not be blamed for a food cost variance she cannot explain
  • Have the closing team trust the prep list without renegotiating it
Frustrations
  • Being asked to justify a variance using a report she has never been shown how to read
  • Systems that assume a keyboard, a mouse, and a quiet moment
  • Advice that ignores the thing she knows and corporate does not — the school playoff on Thursday
  • Having to enter the same number in two places

“I don’t need it to be right every time. I need to know when it’s guessing, so I know which twelve lines to actually look at.”

What makes her abandon the system: being overruled without recourse; a number that is confidently wrong twice in the same week with no visible reason; more than three taps to change one quantity. Design implications: confidence must be visible per line so attention can be rationed (US-06); override must be genuine and fast (US-07); the whole review must fit in ninety seconds and twelve taps (NFR-U2). Served by: US-03, US-05, US-06, US-07, US-09, US-10, US-11, US-12.

Tomás Reyes — Prep Lead  PRIMARY

Represents ~84 people · uses the system at open and at close · executes what Dana confirms

Context of use. Reads the plan at 07:00 from a station display while setting up, and enters waste at close, tired, after a fourteen-hour building day. English is his second language. He works from the printed or displayed list without ever touching the review interface.

What he is actually deciding. Nothing about quantities — he is executing. His decisions are about sequence: what to cut first so the line is ready by 11:00.

Goals
  • Finish prep before the first ticket, every day
  • Cut once — not be told at 10:30 that the number changed
  • Be trusted to flag when a quantity is obviously wrong
Frustrations
  • Quantities in units he has to convert — portions when he works in pounds
  • Waste logging that takes ten minutes at the end of a long shift, so it gets skipped or invented
  • A list that is not in the order the kitchen actually works

“If the sheet says forty portions I have to work out the weight anyway. Just tell me the pounds.”

Why he matters more than he looks: waste data quality — and therefore the accuracy of the model and the credibility of the entire business case — depends on a tired person choosing to record eight lines honestly at 23:15. Every second added to that task erodes the benefit. Design implications: ingredient units, not portions (US-02); station-sequenced list (US-05); pre-populated waste entry with a 90-second median (US-14); accept implausible entries and flag them rather than refusing them (AC-14.4). Served by: US-02, US-05, US-14.

Marcus Bell — District Manager  SECONDARY

Represents 7 people · six restaurants each · the person who makes rollout succeed or fail

Context of use. In a car between locations, on a phone, or in a back office for twenty minutes at a time. He is measured on food cost, guest satisfaction and manager retention across six restaurants simultaneously, and he already receives four reports he does not read.

What he is actually deciding. Where to spend his day. He needs to know which two of six locations need him, and nothing else.

Goals
  • Spot a problem location before it becomes a P&L conversation
  • Get consistency across six kitchens run by six different people
  • Keep his managers — turnover was 31% last year and every departure costs him six weeks
Frustrations
  • Dashboards that show him everything, so he sees nothing
  • Being an approval bottleneck for decisions he has no context on
  • Initiatives that add work to managers who are already the constraint

“Don’t show me six green tiles. Show me the one that’s wrong and let me call them.”

Design implications: exceptions only, on-plan collapsed to a count (US-17); approval queue with the context needed to decide in under a minute, plus delegation and auto-expiry so he is never the reason a kitchen is stuck (US-08, UC-04 flow 3c). Served by: US-08, US-17. Political note: the district manager council is the group that decides whether this initiative is perceived as help or as surveillance. Engagement strategy in D6 treats them as co-designers, not recipients.

Sofia Lindqvist — Director of Data / Data Steward  SECONDARY

Represents 2 people · owns feeds, model governance and the promotion gate

Context of use. At a desk, with the retraining report open, deciding on a Monday morning whether last week’s model version is allowed to serve forty-two restaurants. She is the only person in the organisation who can tell whether the system is quietly getting worse.

What she is actually deciding. Promote or hold. It is a one-bit decision made under time pressure, and it needs a comparison she can defend without reading a notebook.

Goals
  • Never let a worse model reach production
  • Explain any single recommendation from three months ago when finance asks
  • Know within minutes, not days, when a feed has silently degraded
Frustrations
  • Feeds that fail by getting quieter rather than by erroring
  • Being asked to explain a number whose inputs were never retained
  • Business stakeholders reading a 4% accuracy movement as a crisis, or a 15% one as noise

“The dangerous failure isn’t the model breaking. It’s the model still working while the data behind it stops being true.”

Design implications: promotion gate with explicit criteria and no bypass (US-16); 13-month retention of model version, feature snapshot and input hash per line (NFR-M1); freshness monitoring with a 15-minute alert on dead-lettered data (US-15). Served by: US-15, US-16.

Anti-persona: who this system is explicitly not for

Release 1 is not designed for the corporate analyst who wants to explore forecast data interactively, nor for the finance business partner building an ad-hoc margin model. Both are real users with real needs, and both will ask for access in month two. Naming them here as out of scope is what makes that a five-minute conversation rather than a scope negotiation — the same function the Won’t list performs for requirements.

Frequently asked questions

The questions learners ask in week one, answered at the level an architect needs. The Ask assistant draws on these answers directly.

Do I need to become a business analyst?

No. You need to be able to direct one, and to evaluate the output of an AI acting as one. That means holding the seven concepts in section 03, being able to run the ten-point review in section 07, and knowing which figures you must never delegate — the baselines and the definition of success. A BA writes the documents; an architect decides whether the documents are true, complete and buildable, and signs.

What is the single fastest way to judge a requirements document?

Four probes, roughly thirty seconds. Count the Musts — over 60% means prioritisation did not happen. Search the non-functional section for digits — an NFR without a number is not a requirement. Search the whole document for “fallback” or “degraded” — absence means the failure path was never designed. Open the traceability matrix and look for empty cells. Those four catch most of what is wrong with a typical AI-drafted package.

What is the difference between a functional and a non-functional requirement?

A functional requirement says what the system does: “generate a prep quantity for every active item.” A non-functional requirement says how well it must do it, under what constraints: “the nightly batch for 42 locations completes within 20 minutes.” Functional requirements get written by default because the business asks for them directly. Non-functional requirements get forgotten and then get discovered in production at three in the morning. For AI systems, four extra NFRs are mandatory: an accuracy floor with a metric and window, fallback behaviour, explainability, and human override with an audit trail.

How do I write good acceptance criteria?

Use Given/When/Then: Given the preconditions, When the single triggering action, Then the observable, verifiable result. One behaviour per criterion. No conjunctions hiding a second requirement in the When. Observable results only — “the user understands the recommendation” is not observable, “the top three drivers are displayed with their percentage contribution” is. And at least one criterion on the unhappy path, every time. Document D3 has eighteen worked examples.

Why does everything come back marked Must?

Because a language model has no schedule to protect and no budget to spend, so everything it writes feels essential. Re-derive priority yourself with two questions to the sponsor: “if this one item were not ready on go-live day, would you delay the whole launch for it?” and “what does the business do on day one if this is missing?” If there is an answer to the second that does not involve stopping work, it is a Should. In this guide’s worked example the first baseline was 63% Must; after that argument it was 48%, and the demotions are recorded in D8’s change log.

What makes AI requirements different from ordinary software requirements?

Deterministic software fails loudly — it throws an exception. An AI system fails quietly and confidently: it returns 40 pounds where the right answer was 12, in the same format, with the same tone. Because the failure has no signature, the requirement must carry the detection. That means an accuracy threshold with a named metric, grain and measurement window; a defined fallback for when the model or a feed is unavailable; an explanation surface the user can act on; a bounded human override with a reason code and an audit trail; and a promotion gate so a worse model version cannot reach production silently.

What is a traceability matrix actually for?

Three questions nothing else answers. Coverage: is every objective actually being delivered by something? Orphans: is anything being built that no objective asked for? Impact: if this requirement changes, what design, code and tests move with it? In this guide’s example the matrix killed a plausible XML-export requirement that traced to no objective and no stakeholder — eight story points that would have shipped, been maintained for years and never been called.

How much detail is too much?

The test is decision-usefulness, not length. A requirement is detailed enough when a competent developer who has never met the stakeholder can build it, and a tester can prove it. Detail beyond that point is cost without benefit, and it ages badly. The most common failure is not too little detail overall — it is detail evenly spread, with three pages on screen layout and one sentence on what happens when the feed fails.

What should a persona contain, and what should it never contain?

Contain: goals, frustrations, context of use, the decision the person is trying to make, and what makes them abandon the system. Never contain: age, family status, stock photography, or a fictional biography. Knowing that a kitchen manager is 34 with two children changes no design decision. Knowing that she reviews the plan on a grease-splashed tablet at 21:40 with two minutes to spare, and abandons anything requiring more than three taps, changes the entire interface. Personas earn their place when they end arguments: “would Dana do this at 21:40?” is a faster question than “is this good UX?”

How do I stop an AI inventing stakeholders, systems and numbers?

Ground the instruction. Name the real systems that exist and state that no others may be referenced. Supply the real baselines. Then add the instruction that does most of the work: “where you need a fact I have not given you, write ASSUMPTION and record it in the assumptions register.” Finally, demand a self-check — a closing table of every requirement with no test, every objective with no requirement, and every assumption made. Then verify every proper noun in the output against reality before you read the prose.

What is the one thing I must never delegate to the AI?

The baseline numbers and the definition of success. An AI can draft every requirement, use case and test in this guide. It cannot tell you what your waste percentage really is today. If you let it estimate one, every downstream financial figure becomes fiction — fiction that will be quoted back to you by a CFO nine months later, when it is far too late to say it was a placeholder.

How many KPIs should an initiative have?

Between four and eight, each with five parts: definition with the exact formula, measured baseline, target with a date, source system and named owner, and frequency with grain. Mix leading and lagging — leading indicators such as forecast error and plan acceptance are the ones you can steer with weekly; lagging indicators such as food cost percentage are what the board eventually sees. And every efficiency KPI needs a paired quality KPI, or the target will be met in a way nobody wanted: cut waste without watching stockouts and the team will hit the number by running out of food.

Where do use cases fit if I already have user stories?

They are not alternatives. A story is a placeholder for a conversation about one slice of value; a use case is a script for a whole interaction, including its branches. Use stories to plan and slice the build. Use cases where a workflow has multiple actors, system-to-system steps, or failure modes needing explicit handling — which is every AI integration. The discipline in a use case is the alternate and exception flows: in this guide’s example, six use cases carry 31 branches against 40 main-flow steps.

What does “done” mean for a requirement?

Every acceptance criterion has an automated test that fails without the change; the negative criteria are covered, not only the happy path; applicable non-functional thresholds are measured rather than asserted; the traceability row is updated; the behaviour is visible in logs with a correlation ID; and the product owner has accepted it against the criteria as written, not as remembered.

Why does this guide use a restaurant example when I work elsewhere?

Because the structure transfers and the domain does not matter. Substitute claims for menu items and adjusters for kitchen managers and you have an insurance initiative; substitute beds and charge nurses and you have a hospital one. What transfers is the shape: a measured baseline, an objective with an owner and a date, requirements that carry numbers, unhappy paths written down, priorities that were argued over, and a matrix proving nothing is orphaned. Learn the shape here, apply it to your first real project.

Glossary

Terms as used in this guide and in the nine documents. Where an industry definition varies, the definition here is the one that governs these artifacts.

TermDefinition as used here
Acceptance criteriaThe conditions under which a story is agreed to be done, written in Given/When/Then form so that a tester, developer and stakeholder read them identically.
ActorA person, role or system that participates in a use case. Primary actors initiate; secondary actors are called upon.
AssumptionA statement taken as true without proof, on which the plan depends. Every assumption carries a named owner to confirm it and a stated consequence if it is false.
Baseline (measurement)The measured current value of a KPI, with the period over which it was measured. Without it, no benefit can ever be proved.
Baseline (requirements)The agreed, version-controlled set of requirements from which change is measured and against which delivery is accepted.
Bias (forecast)Systematic over- or under-forecasting, measured as mean percentage error. A forecast can hit its error target and still generate waste if it is consistently high.
BOM / bill of materialsThe recipe structure that maps a menu item to its ingredient quantities and yield factors. The bridge between item demand and prep quantity.
BRDBusiness Requirements Document. The controlling requirements baseline for an initiative.
Cold startThe condition of forecasting for an entity with insufficient history. Handled here by a nominated analogue item and scaling factor.
Confidence intervalA stated range within which the true value is expected to fall at a nominal probability. Useless unless its empirical coverage is measured — see calibration, NFR-A3.
Correlation IDAn identifier generated at the entry point of a request or job and propagated through every log line and downstream call, so a failure can be traced from symptom to root cause.
Degraded modeA defined, labelled reduced-capability state entered when a dependency is unavailable, which still produces a usable result. Silence is not a degraded mode.
EpicA body of work too large for one iteration, decomposed into stories. If a story cannot be finished in an iteration, it is an epic wearing a disguise.
Functional requirementA statement of what the system must do, expressed as observable behaviour.
Given/When/ThenThe structure of an acceptance criterion: preconditions, the single triggering action, and the observable result.
GrainThe level at which a measure is computed — item-day, location-week, group-month. Most disagreements between two dashboards are grain disagreements.
IdempotencyThe property that performing an operation twice with the same inputs produces the same end state with no duplicate side effects. Enforced here by the key (location, business date, model version).
INVESTA checklist for story quality: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable.
KPIA measure of success with five mandatory parts: definition, baseline, target and date, source and owner, frequency and grain.
Leading / lagging indicatorLeading indicators move before the outcome and can be steered (forecast error, plan acceptance). Lagging indicators confirm it afterwards (food cost percentage).
MoSCoWPrioritisation into Must, Should, Could and Won’t-this-release. Its value is the argument it forces before the build rather than during it.
Non-functional requirement (NFR)A statement of how well the system must behave — performance, availability, security, accessibility, accuracy, observability — always with a number and a measurement condition.
Orphan requirementA requirement that traces to no objective. Work that will be built, maintained and never justified. Traceability exists chiefly to find these.
Override ledgerThe immutable record of every human adjustment to a system recommendation, with reason code. Both a governance control and a model-improvement input.
PersonaAn evidence-based model of a user expressed through goals, frustrations, context of use and decision authority. Never through demographics.
Promotion gateThe explicit evaluation a new model version must pass before serving production traffic. A gate with a bypass is not a gate.
RACIResponsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed. Accountable must always be exactly one named person.
RTMRequirements Traceability Matrix. Links objective to requirement to design to story to test, proving coverage and the absence of orphans.
StakeholderAnyone who affects, or is affected by, the outcome. Rated on influence and interest, with a position recorded as observed rather than as hoped.
Story pointA relative measure of effort and complexity, not of time. Useful for comparison within a team, meaningless between teams.
TraceabilityThe property that every requirement can be followed forwards to the test that proves it and backwards to the objective that justifies it.
Use caseA script for an interaction: actor, goal, preconditions, numbered main flow, and the alternate and exception flows where the real design decisions live.
User storyA placeholder for a conversation, from the point of view of someone who benefits: as a role, I want a capability, so that a measurable outcome.
WAPEWeighted absolute percentage error — total absolute error divided by total actual volume. Preferred over MAPE for demand forecasting because it is not distorted by low-volume items.
Won’t (this release)An explicit, recorded scope decision rather than a deletion. The most valuable of the four MoSCoW levels, because it is the written record of an argument that would otherwise be re-run monthly.
